Watermarking AI-written text with a secret phrase

Can we hide an invisible mark in text written by an AI, so that later — with nothing but the text and a secret phrase — we can tell it was ours? We built a way to do it, tested how well the mark is found, how much it hurts the text, and how easily it is removed. This page explains the idea and what we found. No background is needed.

The mark is foundAbout 85 of 100 marked texts are caught, while fewer than 1 in 100 normal texts is wrongly flagged — on tasks and secrets that played no part in tuning.
The text is slightly worseA judge prefers the normal answer about 2 to 1, and odd, out-of-place words show up a little more often. Reading examples side by side, the difference is hard to see.
Rewording removes itIf someone has a strong AI model reword the text, the mark is caught only 4–12 times in 100.

How it works, in one minute

Picture a storyteller robot. While it writes, it keeps reaching spots where several words would do equally well — “big” or “large”, “happy” or “glad” — and simply picks one.

We have a secret: a few random words, such as pebble umber echo meadow. And we have a small helper robot. When the helper keeps the secret in mind, some words feel slightly more fitting to it than they otherwise would.

Marking. Whenever the storyteller cannot decide between words, we whisper: take the one the helper likes a bit more. When the storyteller is sure of its word, we stay silent. The story still reads like a normal story — but hundreds of tiny nudges all lean the same way.

Checking. Later we hand a finished text to the helper twice: once with the secret in mind, once without. If the text was ours, the helper-with-the-secret likes its words better far more often than luck allows. If it was not ours, there is no difference. Without the secret, nobody can run this check — and the check needs neither the storyteller nor the question it was answering: only the text, the secret, and the small helper.

The same thing in technical terms

The storyteller is the text generator (here Qwen3.5-0.8B); the helper is a second, small reference model (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct). The reference model reads the text so far twice — after a line containing the secret phrase, and after a neutral line — and for every candidate next word the two readings differ by

d(word) = log p_ref(word | secret phrase, text) − log p_ref(word | neutral line, text)
mark: p′(word) ∝ p_generator(word) · exp( β · clip(d(word), ±0.5) ) β = strength of the nudge check: T = mean(e) / ( sd(e) / √n ), e = clip(d, ±0.5) of each word actually written flagged if fewer than 1 in 100 normal texts score this high

Safeguards while marking: no nudge where the generator is confident; none on the secret's own words; none on a word that would complete a repeated phrase; the decision to stop writing is left untouched. Secrets are 4 or 10 random common words. Tasks: short stories, answers to questions and instructions, and continuing news articles.

What we found

Everything in this section was measured once, on fresh tasks with new secrets, after all choices had been made.

1 · Is the mark found? done

2 · Does the text have more mistakes than normal?

The AI we mark is a small one and makes plenty of mistakes by itself, so the fair question is not “does the marked text have mistakes” but “does it have more than normal text”. We let the judge read normal texts from two separate runs to see what “normal” looks like, including how much it varies by chance, and then compare.

Marked texts are outside the normal range on every line — a real difference, several times larger than the difference between two batches of normal text — but a moderate one: about 15 more texts in 100 contain something that does not belong, roughly half of what our earlier setting caused.

3 · What the difference looks like

We read nine random pairs ourselves, without knowing which text was marked. We guessed right eight times — but mostly with low confidence: both texts usually read like the same small AI. The clearest differences were in news articles, where the normal version stops early and the marked one keeps inventing names and details.

4 · Does it survive rewording? done

No. A capable AI model asked to reword the text removes the mark almost completely. This is the method's main open weakness.

5 · Does the marked AI still follow instructions? done

On tasks whose rules can be checked automatically, marking costs nothing.

How hard can you nudge?

The one setting that matters is the strength of the nudge. On the 60 tuning tasks we tried 14 variants, from gentle to very strong, some with extra protections (“soft start”: no nudging in the first sentence; “topic guard”: never nudge toward words the secret makes much more likely). done

Stronger nudges are caught more often…

…and make the judge prefer the normal answer

Between strength 4 and 6 the text quality drops sharply, because the nudge grows explosively (7 times, then 20, then 55). We therefore chose strength 4 — by a rule written down before looking at these results. A caution: on these tuning tasks strength 4 looked indistinguishable from normal text (0.48); on fresh tasks it scored 0.31. Picking the best of 14 variants on only 60 tasks flatters the winner.

More detail

The trick that made a gentle nudge findable: do not let one loud word decide

Our first check simply averaged how much the helper liked each word. We then found that just 5% of the words carried 91% of the evidence — mostly the first word of an answer and words related to the secret. A check that leans on a few loud words is noisy, and it rewards exactly the words that do not belong in the text. The current check caps how much any single word can count, so the evidence must come from many small preferences. On the very same gently-marked texts this raised the share caught from 53% to 85%.

Does the number of secret words matter?

Yes. Half of our fresh secrets had 4 words, half had 10. With 10 words the gentle nudge was caught more often (90% vs 79%) with no extra damage to the text — a longer secret changes the helper's taste in more words. The price: texts marked with one 10-word secret are also flagged under another 10-word secret about half the time (one in five for 4-word secrets), mainly because our long secrets share words. So longer secrets help to tell whether a text is marked, not whose mark it is. 8 and 16 words are untested.

Long texts, and several secrets taking turns

A long story can carry different secrets in different parts (A, B, C, D, A …). Then any surviving piece still carries a complete mark, and the order of secrets can itself carry information. First run, with the earlier strong setting, a new secret every ~100 words — how often is each part's own secret the best match among the four? done

Earlier phases of the project

We first used the generator itself as the helper. With the strong settings of that time, on 600 fresh tasks: 91% caught at 1 false alarm in 100; the right secret beat 100 decoy secrets 82% of the time while a wrong secret did so no more often than chance. We then showed the helper can be a different model: one that is trained to follow instructions works (Llama-3.2-1B: near-perfect separation of marked from normal text), a raw language model does not. Along the way we found that some early detection rates had been unreliable because the alarm level was set from only 60 normal texts; all numbers on this page use 1,200.

What did not work

Choosing among several sampled continuations instead of nudging word by word (too weak); steering the model's internals (damages text before it marks it); an automatic strength control (it delivers the nudge in bursts and hits the first sentence hardest, but replacing it with a constant nudge of the same total size did not improve quality — what matters is the total); a soft start (no measurable gain); the topic guard (helps a little, not enough to allow stronger nudging); secrets phrased as sentences about a person (the text starts talking about that person).

Limitations
  • Rewording by a capable model removes the mark.
  • The marked AI is small (0.8 billion parameters); the judge gives its normal texts 1.7 out of 5. A stronger AI has more equally-good words to choose from — more room to hide a mark — and more quality to lose. Untested.
  • The check tells that a text is marked more reliably than whose secret marked it: a marked text is flagged under a wrong secret 20–50% of the time. Telling secrets apart needs a second test against decoy secrets.
  • No human ratings; quality rests on two AI judges from different makers, which agree with each other 94% of the time when both take a side.
  • English only. Secrets are research secrets, not cryptography.
Questions you will be asked

How is this different from known “green-list” watermarks? Those derive a random list of favoured words from the preceding words. Here the favoured words come from a language model's reaction to a secret phrase; the secret is readable text; and the model that defines the mark can differ from the model that writes.

Can I check a text without the AI that wrote it? Yes — only the small helper model and the secret are needed.

How often is normal text wrongly flagged? The alarm level is set to 1 in 100 on 1,200 normal texts and checked on others: 0.25% on fresh tasks, 0.9% on short instruction answers, 1–2% on reworded normal text.

How was quality judged? In two separate runs. “Which is better”: two unlabelled answers to the same task, shown in both orders, one question, ties allowed. “What is wrong”: one unlabelled text at a time. Asking both in a single run gave the same results.
